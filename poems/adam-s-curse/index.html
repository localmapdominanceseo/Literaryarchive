Adam's Curse

by W.B. Yeats

Written 1902 Published 1902 Modernism Public Domain

Published in 1902 in In the Seven Woods, this poem describes a conversation between Yeats, a woman, and her beautiful companion, about the labor of making beautiful things — poems, beauty, love itself — and ends with the speaker and the woman sitting in silence, worn out with love.

Quick Answer

"Adam's Curse" is a poem by W.B. Yeats, published in 1902. Three people discuss how much labor goes into making a beautiful poem, into being beautiful, into making love — though the world considers such labor idleness. As the moon rises the speaker and the woman sit in silence, worn out and hollow-hearted, having fallen from the dream of love into its reality.

We sat together at one summer's end,
That beautiful mild woman, your close friend,
And you and I, and talked of poetry.
I said, 'A line will take us hours maybe;
Yet if it does not seem a moment's thought,
Our stitching and unstitching has been naught.
Better go down upon your marrow-bones
And scrub a kitchen pavement, or break stones
Like an old pauper, in all kinds of weather;
For to articulate sweet sounds together
Is to work harder than all these, and yet
Be thought an idler by the noisy set
Of bankers, schoolmasters, and clergymen
The martyrs call the world.'

And thereupon
That beautiful mild woman for whose sake
There's many a one shall find out all heartache
On finding that her voice is sweet and low
Replied, 'To be born woman is to know—
Although they do not talk of it at school—
That we must labour to be beautiful.'

I said, 'It's certain there is no fine thing
Since Adam's fall but needs much labouring.
There have been lovers who thought love should be
So much compounded of high courtesy
That they would sigh and quote with learned looks
Precedents out of beautiful old books;
Yet now it seems an idle trade enough.'

We sat grown quiet at the name of love;
We saw the last embers of daylight die,
And in the trembling blue-green of the sky
A moon, worn as if it had been a shell
Washed by time's waters as they rose and fell
About the stars and broke in days and years.

I had a thought for no one's but your ears:
That you were beautiful, and that I strove
To love you in the old high way of love;
That it had all seemed happy, and yet we'd grown
As weary-hearted as that hollow moon.

Analysis

Form and Structure

Thirty-four lines of loose blank verse with irregular rhymes, the poem's formal relaxation reflecting its subject of labor that looks effortless.

Themes

The paradox that art, beauty, and love all require tremendous labor that must be hidden — the curse of Adam is that nothing comes easily after Eden. The poem is also a quiet elegy for the early stages of Yeats's love for Maud Gonne.

Historical Context

Published in 1902. Yeats wrote the poem partly in response to a conversation with Maud Gonne and her sister. The 'beautiful mild woman' is Maud's sister; Maud herself is implied as the addressee of the final stanzas.

About W.B. Yeats

William Butler Yeats was born on June 13, 1865, near Dublin, Ireland, and became one of the most significant poets of the twentieth century and a central figure in the Irish Literary Revival. His poetry moved from early lyrical, mystically influenced verse toward a sparer, more powerful style concerned with history, aging, and Irish politics. Yeats won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1923 and served as a senator in the Irish Free State. He died in France in 1939.
