Everyone Sang

by Siegfried Sassoon

Written 1919 Published 1919 World War I Poetry Public Domain

Written in April 1919, six months after the Armistice, and published the same year, this poem captures the overwhelming, wordless joy of the war's end — everyone suddenly singing for no reason they can name, wings beating, a ecstasy passing through the world.

Quick Answer

"Everyone Sang" is a poem by Siegfried Sassoon, written in April 1919 after the end of World War I. It describes the sudden, unexplained singing that broke out — everyone singing, startled into song, with wings beating and horror leaving — and ends with the certainty that the singing will continue forever, even after the singer is gone.

Everyone suddenly burst out singing;
And I was filled with such delight
As prisoned birds must find in freedom,
Winging wildly across the white
Orchards and dark-green fields; on — on — and out of sight.

Everyone's voice was suddenly lifted;
And beauty came like the setting sun:
My heart was shaken with tears; and horror
Drifted away . . . O, but Everyone
Was a bird; and the song was wordless; the singing will never be done.

Public domain. Published 1919 in Picture Show.

Analysis

Form and Structure

Eight lines in loose free verse, built around the repeated word 'singing' and the image of wings. The poem is strikingly unlike Sassoon's savage satirical war poems, written in a spirit of relief and joy.

Themes

The overwhelming collective release of the Armistice and the end of years of horror. The singing is involuntary, collective, and almost supernatural — 'everyone's voice was suddenly lifted' without anyone deciding to sing. The poem marks the contrast with Sassoon's bitter wartime verse and represents genuine joy.

Historical Context

Sassoon wrote the poem on April 23, 1919, in a single sitting. It was published in Picture Show (1919) and represents his emotional response to the war's end — a direct contrast to the rage and bitterness of Counter-Attack.

About Siegfried Sassoon

Siegfried Sassoon was born on September 8, 1886, in Brenchley, Kent, England, and became one of the most important poets of World War I, known for bitter, satirical poems attacking the war's conduct and the complacency of those who sent young men to die. He served on the Western Front, won the Military Cross, and then publicly refused to return to duty in 1917 — a protest that was handled by sending him to Craiglockhart War Hospital, where he met Wilfred Owen and shaped his poetry. He died in 1967.
