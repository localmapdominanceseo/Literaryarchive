Tell All the Truth but Tell It Slant

by Emily Dickinson

Written 1872 Published 1945 American Romanticism Full text withheld — rights review

Written around 1872 and published posthumously in 1945, this compact eight-line poem argues that truth must be revealed gradually and indirectly, because its full brightness, like lightning, would blind rather than illuminate.

Quick Answer

"Tell All the Truth but Tell It Slant" is a poem by Emily Dickinson, written around 1872. It argues that truth must be delivered obliquely, at an angle, rather than all at once — because truth's full 'superb surprise' is too dazzling for direct revelation, just as lightning explained kindly to children becomes less frightening, and as too much light blinds rather than enlightens.

Analysis

Form and Structure

Two quatrains in common meter with slant rhyme, with Dickinson's characteristic em-dashes creating pauses that mime the poem's own argument about indirect, gradual revelation.

Themes

The ethics and limits of direct communication. The poem extends to poetry itself: Dickinson suggests that metaphor, indirection, and 'slanting' are not evasions but the only means by which overwhelming truths can be communicated without destroying the recipient. The closing paradox, 'The Truth must dazzle gradually / Or every man be blind,' frames oblique poetic language as a form of protection rather than obscurity.

Historical Context

Dickinson wrote the poem around 1872 but it was not published until 1945, more than sixty years after her death, in a scholarly edition edited by Thomas H. Johnson. It has since become one of her most frequently cited poems on the nature of poetry and truth.

About Emily Dickinson

Emily Dickinson was born on December 10, 1830, in Amherst, Massachusetts, and spent nearly her entire life there. Though she wrote nearly 1,800 poems, fewer than a dozen were published during her lifetime. Her work, marked by unconventional capitalization, slant rhyme, and dashes, was largely discovered and published after her death in 1886. She is now considered one of the most important and original voices in American poetry, known for her meditations on death, immortality, nature, and the inner life.
