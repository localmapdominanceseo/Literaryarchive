Virtue

by George Herbert

Written 1633 Published 1633 Metaphysical Poetry Public Domain

Published in 1633 in The Temple, this short poem addresses a sweet day, a sweet rose, and a sweet spring — noting that each must die — before concluding that only a sweet and virtuous soul lives when all the rest has died, fired into dust.

Quick Answer

"Virtue" is a poem by George Herbert, published in 1633. Each thing he addresses — the sweet day, the sweet rose, the sweet spring — must close and die. Only a sweet and virtuous soul, like seasoned timber, does not end: the whole world turns to coal, and the soul lives on.

Sweet day, so cool, so calm, so bright,
The bridal of the earth and sky:
The dew shall weep thy fall to-night;
For thou must die.

Sweet rose, whose hue angry and brave
Bids the rash gazer wipe his eye:
Thy root is ever in its grave,
And thou must die.

Sweet spring, full of sweet days and roses,
A box where sweets compacted lie;
My music shows ye have your closes,
And all must die.

Only a sweet and virtuous soul,
Like seasoned timber, never gives;
But though the whole world turn to coal,
Then chiefly lives.

Public domain. Published 1633 in The Temple.

Analysis

Form and Structure

Four quatrains of alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter with ABAB rhyme, ending each stanza with a refrain line about dying, until the final stanza breaks the pattern.

Themes

The transience of natural beauty versus the permanence of a virtuous soul. Each natural thing is sweet but doomed; the soul seasoned by virtue outlasts them all.

Historical Context

Published in The Temple (1633), Herbert's posthumous collection. One of the most anthologized of Herbert's short lyrics for its formal elegance and clarity of argument.

About George Herbert

George Herbert was born on April 3, 1593, in Montgomery, Wales, and became one of the leading Metaphysical poets, known for devotional verse of striking formal ingenuity. He served as a Church of England priest in his final years and prepared his entire collection, The Temple, for posthumous publication before his death in 1633. His poetry combines intense personal piety with wit and formal experimentation.
