London

by William Blake

Written 1794 Published 1794 English Romanticism Public Domain

Published in 1794 in Songs of Experience, this poem describes a walk through the streets of London, hearing in every voice and face the marks of weakness and woe — the cries of chimney sweeps, the sighs of soldiers, the curses of harlots — and identifying the source of all this suffering in 'mind-forged manacles.'

Quick Answer

"London" is a poem by William Blake, published in 1794 in Songs of Experience. Walking through chartered (controlled, owned) streets along the chartered Thames, the speaker hears in every face and voice marks of weakness and woe. He identifies the cries of chimney sweeps, the sighs of soldiers dying for the king, and the curses of young harlots as evidence of a society whose suffering is produced by mental as much as physical chains.

I wander through each chartered street,
Near where the chartered Thames does flow.
And mark in every face I meet
Marks of weakness, marks of woe.

In every cry of every Man,
In every Infant's cry of fear,
In every voice, in every ban,
The mind-forged manacles I hear

How the Chimney-sweeper's cry
Every blackening Church appals;
And the hapless Soldier's sigh
Runs in blood down Palace walls

But most through midnight streets I hear
How the youthful Harlot's curse
Blasts the new-born Infant's tear,
And blights with plagues the Marriage hearse.

Public domain. Text per the Academy of American Poets (poets.org).

Analysis

Form and Structure

Four quatrains of iambic tetrameter with ABAB rhyme, formally controlled in a way that mirrors the 'chartered' streets the poem describes — everything ordered, regulated, owned.

Themes

Social and political oppression, and the role of mental conditioning in sustaining it. The 'mind-forged manacles' is Blake's central image: the chains that keep London's poor in suffering are not only physical laws and economic exploitation but internalized beliefs that make people accept their condition as natural or inevitable. The chimney sweep's cry 'appals' the church that owns the children's labor; the soldier's sigh 'runs in blood down Palace walls.'

Historical Context

Published in Songs of Experience (1794), paired against the more innocent vision of Songs of Innocence (1789). London in 1794 was a city of extreme inequality, with children working as chimney sweeps, prostitution driven by poverty, and soldiers dying in wars against revolutionary France. Blake's radicalism here is political as much as spiritual.

About William Blake

William Blake was born on November 28, 1757, in London, England, and worked as a poet, painter, and printmaker, producing illustrated volumes of poetry that he engraved, printed, and hand-colored himself. Largely unrecognized during his lifetime, he is now considered one of the founding figures of English Romanticism. His best-known collection, Songs of Innocence and of Experience, pairs poems of childlike wonder with darker counterparts exploring suffering and moral complexity. He died in London in 1827.
