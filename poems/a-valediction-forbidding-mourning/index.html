A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning

by John Donne

Written 1611 Published 1633 Metaphysical Poetry Public Domain

Written around 1611, reportedly as Donne was leaving for a journey to Europe, and published posthumously in 1633, this poem argues that true spiritual love does not need physical proximity — and uses the famous compass conceit to describe two souls that, however far they move apart, remain joined at the center.

Quick Answer

"A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning" is a poem by John Donne, published posthumously in 1633. As the speaker prepares to leave his beloved, he asks her not to mourn: their love is not dependent on the senses and does not need proximity. He compares their two souls to the two legs of a mathematical compass — one leg fixed at the center, the other moving out, yet always joined and always returning.

As virtuous men pass mildly away,
And whisper to their souls to go,
Whilst some of their sad friends do say,
The breath goes now, and some say, No:

So let us melt, and make no noise,
No tear-floods, nor sigh-tempests move;
'Twere profanation of our joys
To tell the laity our love.

Moving of th' earth brings harms and fears,
Men reckon what it did, and meant;
But trepidation of the spheres,
Though greater far, is innocent.

Dull sublunary lovers' love
(Whose soul is sense) cannot admit
Absence, because it doth remove
Those things which elemented it.

But we, by a love so much refined
That ourselves know not what it is,
Inter-assured of the mind,
Care less, eyes, lips, and hands to miss.

Our two souls therefore, which are one,
Though I must go, endure not yet
A breach, but an expansion,
Like gold to airy thinness beat.

If they be two, they are two so
As stiff twin compasses are two;
Thy soul, the fixed foot, makes no show
To move, but doth, if th' other do.

And though it in the centre sit,
Yet when the other far doth roam,
It leans and hearkens after it,
And grows erect, as that comes home.

Such wilt thou be to me, who must,
Like th' other foot, obliquely run;
Thy firmness makes my circle just,
And makes me end where I begun.

Analysis

Form and Structure

Nine quatrains of iambic tetrameter with an ABAB rhyme scheme, the poem's formal regularity mirroring the constancy it argues for.

Themes

The superiority of spiritual over physical love: because their love is based on souls rather than sense, separation is not a catastrophe but merely a temporary extension. The compass conceit is the poem's centerpiece — the fixed foot (the beloved) leans toward the moving foot (Donne) and grows erect when he returns, describing the whole geometry of their relationship.

Historical Context

Izaak Walton, Donne's biographer, reports that Donne gave the poem to his wife Anne before leaving for France in 1611 — though scholars debate the exact occasion. It was published posthumously in 1633 and is now considered the finest example of the Metaphysical conceit.

About John Donne

John Donne was born in 1572 in London, England, and became the leading figure among the Metaphysical poets, a group known for intellectual wit, elaborate conceits, and the fusion of intense feeling with rigorous argument. After an early career writing love poetry, Donne was ordained as an Anglican priest in 1615 and eventually became Dean of St. Paul's Cathedral in London, where he was renowned for his sermons. Much of his poetry, including his Holy Sonnets, circulated only in manuscript during his lifetime and was first published after his death in 1631.
