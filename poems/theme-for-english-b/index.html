Theme for English B

by Langston Hughes

Written 1949 Published 1951 Harlem Renaissance Full text withheld — rights review

Published in 1951 in Montage of a Dream Deferred, this poem is a response to an instructor's assignment to write a page that 'comes out of you' — and the speaker, the only Black student in the class, reflects on what comes out of him and what it means to be both part of America and excluded from it.

Quick Answer

"Theme for English B" is a poem by Langston Hughes, published in 1951. An instructor assigns a page that 'comes out of you — then it will be true.' The speaker, the only Black student in the class at Columbia, reflects on what is true about himself: he likes the same things as others, he is part of the instructor who is part of him, they are part of America — though that is not always easy for either of them.

Analysis

Form and Structure

Free verse in the conversational, autobiographical mode Hughes developed in his later work, incorporating the speaker's specific biographical details (Harlem, Columbia, age twenty-two) to ground the poem's universal argument in particular reality.

Themes

The impossibility of a purely individual voice — the speaker's page comes out of him, but he is made of his context, his city, his race, his age, his instructor, his America. Identity is always relational, and in America, that relationship is shaped by race.

Historical Context

Published in Montage of a Dream Deferred (1951), the collection built around Harlem jazz and blues structures. The poem is autobiographical — Hughes did attend Columbia University — and engages directly with Du Bois's double consciousness.

About Langston Hughes

Langston Hughes was born on February 1, 1901, in Joplin, Missouri, and became one of the leading voices of the Harlem Renaissance, the flowering of African American literature, art, and music centered in New York City during the 1920s. Hughes drew on the rhythms of jazz and blues music to develop a distinctive poetic voice that celebrated Black culture and identity while confronting American racism directly. He died in New York City in 1967.
